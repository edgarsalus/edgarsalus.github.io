Getting a wind turbine to start in city windGetting a wind turbine to start in city wind

A 6DOF CFD study of how blade geometry decides how fast a small Savonius turbine spins up from rest in slow, gusty city wind.

    01 · Question

    The question

    Crossing Berlin’s Oberbaum Bridge, I noticed wind funneled along the Spree going to waste. City wind is slow, turbulent and changes direction, so a turbine there has to start quickly from any side. That points to a drag-⁠based Savonius rotor.

    • 4.1 m/sBerlin’s mean wind speed
    • HourlyHow often the wind changes direction
    • 150 sTest window: between short gusts (~30 s) and steady wind

    02

    How a Savonius rotor turns

    1. 01

      Concave side

      Large drag

      Wind fills the hollow of one blade.

    2. 02

      Convex side

      Small drag

      Air slips around the curved back of the other.

    3. 03

      Difference

      Net torque

      The imbalance turns the rotor about its spine.

    • α=T/IAngular acceleration = torque ÷ inertia
    • P=T·ωPower = torque × angular velocity

    Every change that adds torque must be weighed against the inertia it adds.

    03

    Method

    1. 01

      Autodesk Fusion

      Model

      CAD for every variant, with its moment of inertia.

    2. 02

      Steady CFD

      Screen

      Rank designs by drag difference ÷ inertia.

    3. 03

      Transient 6DOF CFD

      Spin up

      The rotor accelerates from rest for 150 s.

    4. 04

      Ansys Fluent

      Compare

      Angular velocity, torque and power vs. the baseline.

    The six-degree-of-freedom solver lets each rotor spin up under its own torque instead of at a prescribed speed. Cheap steady runs filtered designs before the expensive transient ones.

    Simulation setup10 parameters
    Rotor4.2 m diameter, 3 m tall (3.4 m with spine), 0.4 m spine
    MaterialSteel, hollowed to 10 mm walls; moment of inertia from CAD for every variant
    Domain5 diameters downstream, 2 upstream
    MeshFluent watertight workflow; growth rate 1.1, size 0.02, curvature normal angle 8°
    Inflow4.1 m/s, 30% turbulence intensity, viscosity ratio 10
    Transient modelRealizable k-ε with enhanced wall treatment (y+ < 3 is hard to keep everywhere with rotating parts)
    Rotor motion6DOF solver: the rotor spins up under its own torque
    Time stepping0.05 s steps, 3,000+ steps, at least 20 iterations each: 60,000+ iterations per run
    ScreeningSteady transition SST (four equations), 200 iterations, ranked by drag difference ÷ moment of inertia
    OutputsAngular velocity ω, torque T, power P = T · ω

    04

    The baseline spins up in waves

    The standard two-blade rotor never reached a steady speed. As its hollow side starts moving with the wind, it catches less of it, so torque rises and falls.

    After 150 s it had reached 0.814 rad/s and 58.9 W.

    05

    Four levers, one at a time

    06

    The surprise

    Starting the “wrong” way was faster

    Started turned 90°, blades parallel to the wind, the rotor should have been slower. It wasn’t.

    • 0.925 rad/sAngular velocity
    • 68.9 WPower, +17%
    • >70%Earlier first speed peak

    Air followed the curve of the leading blade (the Coandă effect), which acted as a guide vane into the trailing blade.

    07

    Putting it together

    The final rotor combined v3 end plates, a 1.1 m blade depth and a 20 cm peak offset.

    • +16%Peak torque of the final design
    • +0.7%Peak angular velocity
    • +4%Power after 150 s
    • +5%Moment of inertia: the catch

    08 · Takeaway

    Takeaway

    Improvements measured one at a time don’t simply add up: responsiveness, from low inertia, can matter more than peak torque.

    09

    Limits and next steps

    Limitations

    • Startup only: no rotor reached steady speed
    • One wind direction per run
    • Wall thickness not structurally verified
    • One-at-a-time screening misses interactions
    • Simulation only, no prototype

    Next steps

    • Lighter composite blades
    • Blades designed for flow attachment
    • Finer 5 cm increments
    • Wake and array studies
    • Optimal rotor size for cities
